Strata and strata management

Strata management is the day to day administration of a strata scheme on behalf of its owners corporation. It matters because the owners corporation has legal duties under the Strata Schemes Management Act 2015: to maintain and repair common property, insure the building, hold its meetings, keep records and manage its funds. Strata management is how those duties get done properly and on the record. When it slips, the cost shows up in levies, disputes and repairs left too long.

An introduction to strata management

They are the work an owners corporation hands to its strata manager under a management agreement. The core is financial: trust accounting, levy notices and arrears, budgets, invoices and reporting. Around that sit meetings and minutes, the strata roll and records, insurance renewals and claims, repairs and contractors, and by-law notices. What is included varies between agencies, so compare full schedules of services, not just the fee. Ours sets out in full what the management fee covers.

A strata company is a business licensed to act as a strata managing agent, appointed by an owners corporation to run the administration of its scheme. It acts as the owners corporation's agent, using the functions delegated to it in the management agreement. In practice that means the finances, meetings, records, insurance and repairs. The company does the work and gives advice, while the owners corporation still makes the decisions through its general meetings and strata committee.

The owners corporation appoints a licensed strata managing agent at a general meeting and delegates functions to it through a management agreement. That agreement sets the services, the fees, the term and how it can be ended. The manager then runs the scheme day to day, within the budget the owners have approved. Decisions the Strata Schemes Management Act 2015 reserves for the owners, such as budgets, levies and by-law changes, are still made at a general meeting.

Because the obligations never stop, and strata committees are owners giving up their own time. Every year an owners corporation has to set budgets and levies, keep proper accounts and records, insure the building, maintain common property, meet its fire safety obligations and hold its meetings on time. A strata manager keeps all of that moving and keeps the evidence that it was done. The committee still makes the decisions, without carrying the administration.

No. NSW law does not require an owners corporation to appoint a strata manager, and some smaller schemes manage themselves. The obligations are the same either way. A self managed scheme still has to hold its meetings, keep its records, insure the building, maintain common property and account for its funds under the Strata Schemes Management Act 2015. Most schemes appoint a licensed manager because doing all of that properly, year after year, takes more time and specialist knowledge than a volunteer committee has to spare.

Managers, committees and roles

A strata manager runs the day to day administration of your scheme on behalf of the owners corporation. That covers the money, levy notices, budgets, trust accounts and reporting, the meetings and minutes, the records and the strata roll, insurance and claims, contractors and maintenance, and compliance with the Strata Schemes Management Act 2015.

The decisions stay with the owners corporation. Carrying them out, and keeping the evidence that they were carried out, is the manager's job.

The owners corporation is responsible for the strata scheme as a whole, whether or not it appoints a strata manager. Under the Strata Schemes Management Act 2015 it must maintain and repair common property, insure the building, keep an administrative fund and a capital works fund, set levies, hold an annual general meeting and keep proper records. It also makes and enforces the by-laws. A manager can carry out much of the work, but the legal responsibility stays with the owners corporation.

What an owners corporation is

A strata manager runs the administration of the scheme, and a building manager runs the building itself, on site. The strata manager handles the finances, levies, meetings, records, insurance and compliance under a management agreement with the owners corporation. A building manager or caretaker supervises cleaning, contractors, access, security and daily operations, usually under a separate agreement. Larger schemes often have both, and they work best when instructions, contractors and budgets line up.

The owners corporation makes the decisions, either at a general meeting of owners or through the strata committee it elects. The biggest decisions belong to a general meeting, including budgets and levies, by-law changes and appointing a strata manager, and some, such as changes to the by-laws, need a special resolution. The strata committee, elected at each annual general meeting, makes many of the decisions in between. The strata manager acts on those decisions, within the authority the owners corporation has delegated.

Levies, fees and payments

A strata levy is the contribution each lot owner pays to fund the owners corporation. Levies go into two funds. The administrative fund pays for running costs such as insurance, cleaning, common area electricity, routine repairs and management fees, and the capital works fund pays for larger, longer term work such as painting, roof replacement and lift upgrades. Each lot's share is set by its unit entitlement, which is recorded on the strata plan.

Strata levies explained

The committee and manager propose them, and the owners decide at the annual general meeting. The proposal is a budget estimating what the administrative fund needs for the year ahead and what the capital works fund needs, drawing on the scheme's ten year capital works fund plan. Once the owners approve it, each lot's share follows its unit entitlement. If an unexpected cost comes up during the year, a general meeting can raise a special levy.

Levies are how an owners corporation pays for everything the building needs. They fund insurance, repairs, cleaning, compliance and management, and they build the capital works fund that pays for major works later. When levies are set too low, maintenance gets deferred and the shortfall usually returns as a special levy, often at short notice. Adequate levies protect the building, and with it the value of every lot.

Because the costs behind them do. Insurance premiums have risen sharply across NSW, contractor rates have followed, and buildings get older rather than younger, which means more maintenance and larger capital works.

A levy that never rises is usually a capital works fund quietly running out. We would rather show your committee the ten year position and let owners decide with real numbers in front of them.

The owners corporation is responsible for levies, and where a strata manager is appointed, the manager collects and administers them on its behalf. That means issuing levy notices, receipting payments into trust, keeping the levy register up to date, following up arrears and reporting the position to the committee. The money belongs to the owners corporation, never to the manager. We reconcile banking daily, and owners can check their levy position in the portal at any hour.

About the owner portal

Levies are usually paid in quarterly instalments, set when the owners approve the budget at the annual general meeting. Each owner receives a levy notice before every instalment falls due, showing the amount, the fund it goes to and the due date. Special levies follow their own schedule, set by the general meeting that approves them. We offer a range of payment options, so owners can pay in the way that suits them.

The owners corporation can charge interest and take steps to recover the debt. Under the Strata Schemes Management Act 2015, overdue levies can attract simple interest, and continued arrears can be recovered through the courts, together with reasonable recovery costs. An owner in arrears also generally cannot vote at general meetings. Under our fee structure, the cost of an overdue levy notice or recovery instructions is paid by the owner concerned, not the rest of the building. If you are having trouble paying, talk to your manager early.

It depends on how the lot is used, so confirm your position with your accountant. For an owner occupier, levies are generally a private cost and not deductible. For an investor, levies on a lot that is rented, or genuinely available for rent, are generally deductible, although a special levy raised for a particular capital project may be treated differently. If we manage your building, your portal keeps a record of every levy you have paid.

Repairs, maintenance and responsibility

The owners corporation, funded through levies, under the Strata Schemes Management Act 2015. Common property generally covers the structure and the shared parts of the building: external walls, the roof, common area floors, lifts, and shared services. Anything inside your lot is generally yours, unless a registered by-law says otherwise.

Where responsibility is not obvious, we check the strata plan and the registered by-laws rather than guess, and we tell you which it is before any work is arranged.

What counts as common property

It depends on whether the item is common property or part of a lot. Under the Strata Schemes Management Act 2015, the owners corporation maintains and repairs common property, and each owner looks after their own lot. The strata plan sets the boundary, and a by-law can pass responsibility for particular items to an owner, often where an owner has been approved to alter common property. As a general guide, the structure and shared services are common property, and fittings inside the lot are the owner's.

How we run a repair

In most NSW strata schemes, the owners corporation, because the roof is usually common property. That covers the roof structure, tiles or sheeting, membranes, flashings and gutters, paid for from levies. The exceptions come from the strata plan or a registered by-law, for example a townhouse scheme where a by-law passes roof maintenance to each lot owner. If water is coming through your ceiling, report it to your strata manager straight away rather than engaging your own contractor.

The owners corporation can be in breach of its duty to maintain and repair common property, and owners can act on it. Section 106 of the Strata Schemes Management Act 2015 sets that duty, and lets an owner claim reasonably foreseeable loss caused by a breach. Start by putting the problem to your strata manager or committee in writing. If that does not resolve it, NSW Fair Trading offers mediation, and the NSW Civil and Administrative Tribunal, known as NCAT, can order the work to be done.

Strata insurance

Strata insurance is the building insurance an owners corporation is required to hold under the Strata Schemes Management Act 2015. The building must be insured for its full replacement value, and the owners corporation must also hold public liability insurance and any workers compensation cover the law requires. The premium is paid from levies. Your strata manager arranges quotes, renews the policy on the owners corporation's instructions and lodges claims.

Strata insurance explained

It covers the building and common property against damage, and the owners corporation's legal liability. A typical policy covers the structure, roof, external and internal walls, floors, ceilings, lifts, shared plumbing and electrical services, and built in fixtures, against events such as fire and storm. Public liability cover protects the owners corporation if someone is injured on common property. Policies differ in what they include, so read the schedule rather than relying on a summary.

Your own contents, and anything the policy excludes. Strata insurance covers the building, so your furniture, belongings and personal liability inside your lot sit outside it. Policies also generally exclude wear and tear, gradual deterioration and damage caused by poor maintenance, and most claims carry an excess. Exclusions differ between insurers, so read the policy wording, and ask your manager for a copy of the current policy if you do not have one.

Yes, you should hold your own cover. Strata insurance covers the building and common property, not what is inside your lot or your personal liability there. Owner occupiers generally need contents insurance with liability cover, and investors generally need landlord insurance, which can cover loss of rent and damage by tenants. Check what the strata policy already includes, so you insure the gaps rather than paying twice.

The owners corporation pays the premium, and every owner contributes through their levies. The premium is budgeted in the administrative fund each year, and each lot's share generally follows its unit entitlement. Premiums are a large part of most budgets, so how the policy is placed matters. We appoint a broker, arrange quotes, report on the renewal to the committee and renew on the owners corporation's instructions.

Disputes and resolution

Most strata disputes come from a short list: noise, parking, pets, renovations, repairs, levies and the use of common property. Underneath, the cause is usually a by-law that is unclear or unevenly enforced, or a decision that owners did not understand. Many start as poor communication rather than a real disagreement. Clear by-laws, prompt answers and minutes that record what was actually decided stop a lot of them before they start.

Put it in writing to your strata manager, setting out the problem and the outcome you want. Include your lot number, dates, photos and any earlier correspondence. The manager deals with it or takes it to the strata committee, and if it needs a decision from the owners, you can ask for a motion to go on the agenda of the next general meeting. We answer correspondence within 24 hours.

The next step is mediation through NSW Fair Trading, and if that does not work, an application to the NSW Civil and Administrative Tribunal, known as NCAT. Mediation is informal and confidential, and a mediator helps both sides reach an agreement. For most strata applications, NCAT requires mediation to have been attempted first. It can make binding orders under the Strata Schemes Management Act 2015, including orders to carry out repairs, orders about by-laws and orders settling disputes about how the scheme is run.

Choosing or changing a strata manager

Choose on evidence rather than on the lowest fee. Check the agency's licence with NSW Fair Trading, then ask how many schemes each manager carries, who answers after hours emergencies, how quickly correspondence is answered and what the reports look like. Ask for a full schedule of services with every quote, so you can see what the fee covers and what costs extra. Then ask a current client committee how the agency performs when something goes wrong.

Questions to ask a new strata manager

Look for systems you can check, not adjectives. That means a person who answers when you call, an emergency line answered at any hour, correspondence answered within a stated time, portal access for every owner, reports written for owners rather than accountants, and flat fees set against a full schedule of services. Ask about experience with buildings like yours, whether residential, commercial or mixed use. Most of all, ask what happens when something goes wrong, and who follows it through.

Laws and compliance

Strata in NSW is governed mainly by the Strata Schemes Management Act 2015 and the Strata Schemes Management Regulation 2016. The Act sets the rules for how schemes are run, including meetings, levies, repairs, insurance, by-laws, strata managers and disputes, and the Regulation fills in the detail. The Strata Schemes Development Act 2015 covers creating, changing and ending schemes. Strata managing agents are licensed under the Property and Stock Agents Act 2002, and building work brings in planning, building and fire safety laws as well.

NSW strata legislation explained

The Strata Schemes Management Act 2015 is the main NSW law on how strata schemes are run. It took effect in November 2016, replacing the Strata Schemes Management Act 1996, and sets the rules for owners corporations, strata committees, meetings, levies and funds, repairs, insurance, by-laws, strata managing agents and dispute resolution. It has been amended since, so read the current version. NSW Fair Trading provides guidance and mediation under it, and the NSW Civil and Administrative Tribunal decides the disputes that mediation does not resolve.

On the NSW Legislation website, legislation.nsw.gov.au, which publishes the current version of every Act and Regulation with amendments included. NSW Fair Trading explains the strata laws in plain English and publishes updates when they change. The NSW Civil and Administrative Tribunal's website explains how strata disputes are heard. If you are relying on the law for a decision, check the version in force at the time, or ask a strata lawyer.
